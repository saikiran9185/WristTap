# Stop integrating: let your arm and touches fix drift

You cannot fix IMU drift by doing the maths better. You fix it by never letting the error build up. For WristTap that means three things. First, do not turn acceleration into position by "double integration" (adding up acceleration twice). That error grows to metres within seconds. Instead, work out where the hand is from the *angles* of the upper arm and forearm plus their lengths, like a drawing compass. Researchers call this forward kinematics. Its error is about **2 cm right after calibration**, and it grows slowly and in a straight line instead of exploding. Second, almost all of the remaining drift is one number: the arm's left-right compass direction, called heading or yaw. Each degree of heading error moves the fingertip about **1.2 cm** on a wall at arm's length. Third, every touch on the wall is a free "you are here" signal. The four-corner calibration and each tap on a known button can reset that heading number, so the error stays near the calibration level instead of growing. The cheapest, most effective path costs almost nothing: good calibration, an open-source orientation filter called VQF, the arm-angle model, and touch-based resets. Swapping the MPU6500 for an ICM-45686 or LSM6DSV (about $6–7 each) is the best hardware upgrade. Deep learning is the newest option, but on its own it gets only about **7–10 cm**. It works best as a small add-on, not the core. Nobody has published this exact wall-touch setup, so WristTap will have to measure its own final accuracy. The numbers below are best estimates built from similar systems.

## Double integration fails in seconds, so position must come from angles

An IMU (inertial measurement unit) has two sensors. The accelerometer measures acceleration, including gravity. The gyroscope ("gyro") measures how fast the sensor is turning. Every gyro has a small false reading even when it is perfectly still. This is called **bias**. The MPU6500 datasheet allows a bias of up to **±5 °/s** at room temperature. That bias can shift by about **±0.24 °/s for every 1 °C** of temperature change ([MPU-6500 datasheet](https://ocw.cs.pub.ro/courses/_media/pm/prj2017/amocanu/mpu_6500_rev1.0_1_.pdf)). A band warming up on your skin can therefore change its own bias while you use it.

Bias hurts angles and position very differently. A constant gyro bias makes the angle error grow in a straight line over time. Position from double integration is far worse. Accelerometer bias makes position error grow with time squared. Gyro bias is worse still, because a slightly wrong tilt makes part of gravity look like sideways motion. Cambridge's standard tutorial shows that a tilt error of just **0.05° creates 7.7 m of position error after 30 s**. In 500 tests with a still, commercial-grade IMU, pure integration was off by **more than 150 m after 60 s** on average ([Woodman 2007](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/techreports/UCAM-CL-TR-696.pdf)). Learned inertial odometry, which uses neural networks to clean up this integration, still drifts by **3.5–6.5% of the distance travelled** on a wearable dataset ([CVPR 2026 MARIO supplement](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2026F/supplemental/Li_MARIO_Motion-Augmented_Real-Time_CVPRF_2026_supplemental.pdf)). It also relies on the rhythm of walking, which a reaching arm does not have. The verdict is clear. Never use double integration as your main source of hand position.

Angles are much better behaved. The accelerometer always knows which way gravity points, so each segment's up-down tilt (pitch and roll) never drifts. Only heading drifts, because nothing in a 6-axis IMU can sense compass direction ([NXP community](https://community.nxp.com/t5/Sensors/Gyro-drifting/m-p/435989)). If you know the upper-arm angle, the forearm angle and the two lengths, you can place the hand with no integration at all. A 2021 *Scientific Reports* study used exactly this two-IMU arm model. It measured wrist position relative to the shoulder with an error of **0.09 ± 1.81 cm** against a Vicon camera system, about as good as an HTC Vive tracker (0.48 ± 1.58 cm) ([Sci Rep 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-01805-2)). For comparison, multi-IMU arm models reach about **1–2% of the range of motion**, while a single wrist IMU that relies on still moments gets below 10% ([PMC9823748](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9823748); [PoliTo thesis](https://webthesis.biblio.polito.it/34849/1/tesi.pdf)). Your setup (one IMU on the wrist, one on the upper arm) is already the right shape.

## One drifting number causes most of the error

With an arm-angle model, the error breaks down into a few simple parts. Two parts are already solved. Tilt is fixed by gravity. The *relative* heading between forearm and upper arm can be held in check by the elbow, because the elbow bends mostly like a hinge. A group led by Thomas Seel and Daniel Laidig showed that two 6-axis IMUs across a joint can keep their relative angle stable for a long time **with no magnetometer**. Errors stayed **under 4°** on a test joint ([Lehmann et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.00639)). A later version even works out the joint axes by itself from ordinary movement ([Laidig et al. 2022](https://cris.fau.de/publications/287478627)).

What is left is the **common heading**: the whole arm slowly swinging left or right compared with the wall. Nothing on your body can measure this directly. This is the main job for wall touches. The geometry is simple. With a shoulder-to-fingertip reach of about 0.7 m, **1° of heading error moves the fingertip about 1.2 cm, and 5° moves it about 6 cm** ([arm-chain analysis based on Sci Rep 2021 setup](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-01805-2)). How fast heading drifts depends on the sensor and the filter. Hobby setups report about **1.8 °/min for an MPU6050** ([Parallax forum](https://forums.parallax.com/uploads/attachments/41167/106673.pdf)) and **0.5 °/min for the BNO086's no-magnetometer mode** ([SparkFun BNO086](https://www.sparkfun.com/products/retired/14686)). Without corrections, that works out to a few centimetres of sideways creep per minute. That is slow enough for touches to keep fixing it.

The body adds one more error. The arm model assumes the shoulder stays put. If you twist your torso by 5°, the shoulder shifts about 1.6 cm, and the whole arm's heading also turns 5°, about 6 cm at the fingertip. A chest IMU measures torso twist and lean, which is why shoulder studies routinely add a thorax sensor ([PMC12250904](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12250904/)). No IMU can sense small body *shifts*, like swaying or a half step. Commercial suits like Xsens handle this using feet touching the ground. Movella (Xsens's maker) admits drift is worse when the body is not touching anything ([Movella blog](https://movella.com/resources/blog/mocap-without-limits-how-xsens-solved-the-drift-dilemma)). For WristTap, a floor marker plus touch resets is the practical answer.

## Every touch on the wall is a free position fix

Robots already use this trick. When a robot's foot touches the ground, the robot knows that point is not moving. That fact goes into its filter as an extra "measurement". A filter is the maths that blends sensor readings with predictions. The best-known kind is the Kalman filter, and an "EKF" is a version for curved, real-world problems. Hartley and colleagues showed that on a walking robot, contact events make absolute position and some rotation measurable. Their "invariant EKF" beat a standard filter ([Hartley et al.](https://arxiv.com/abs/1805.10410)). Foot-mounted trackers use the same idea, called **ZUPT** (zero-velocity update): every step, the foot is briefly still, so speed is reset to zero ([OpenShoe](https://openshoe.org)). In arm tracking for stroke rehab, ZUPT was the most effective drift fix tested ([Kent study](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/99941/1/article.pdf)). IMU pens use the writing surface as a flat-plane constraint and reach about **1.84 mm** accuracy on small letters ([Bu et al.](https://bohrium.dp.tech/paper/arxiv/2101.06022)).

A WristTap touch can give three different corrections, and they are not equally strong. Every touch says "the fingertip is on the wall plane". That fixes depth (distance from the wall), but not left-right position. Every touch also says "the hand is still for a moment". During that pause the gyro should read zero, so the filter can re-measure gyro bias. That slows future drift. A touch on a **known target**, like a projected button, says "the fingertip is exactly here". That is the strong one. It fixes the drifting heading number and the shoulder position. So the interface design matters. Big buttons at known spots, and an occasional "tap here to continue", double as drift resets. Touches at unknown spots only fix depth. No published paper uses wrist-IMU wall touches as position fixes for an arm model. This combination appears new, though each piece is well proven ([arm-kinematics note sources: Hartley](https://arxiv.com/abs/1805.10410); [Movella](https://movella.com/resources/blog/mocap-without-limits-how-xsens-solved-the-drift-dilemma)).

The four-corner calibration fits the same framework. At each corner you know the IMU angles and the corner's position on the wall. That gives 12 numbers (4 corners × 3 directions). The unknowns are about 6–8: shoulder position (3), heading offset (1), the two arm lengths (2), and maybe small sensor tilts. More equations than unknowns means a standard "least squares" fit can solve them all. Least squares finds the values that make all the measurements fit best. If you do not know the projection size in metres, fit a flat plane through the four fingertip points. Then map it to projector pixels with a 4-point "homography", a standard corner-to-corner warp. This also soaks up arm-length errors. Calibration methods based on real joint positions beat simple "hold a pose" calibration (**5–8° vs worse, p < 0.001**) ([PMC12250904](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12250904/)). Corner touches are this better kind of calibration, tailored to your task. Pen systems find their writing plane the same way, by fitting a plane to the points ([CERIST](https://biblio.cerist.dz/show/fr/00000000000000619604000000)).

## Better chips and filters slow the drift; magnets and big networks mostly do not

**Filters.** Classic filters (complementary, Mahony, Madgwick) all use gravity to fix tilt, and studies disagree on which one wins ([Ludwig & Burnham 2018](https://web.cs.ndsu.nodak.edu/~siludwig/Publish/papers/ICUAS2018.pdf)). The current best free option is **VQF** (Laidig & Seel, 2023). It notices when the sensor is resting and re-measures gyro bias, both at rest and while moving. Across public test datasets it averaged **2.9° error, against 5.3–16.7° for eight older filters**. It is MIT-licensed, with C++ and Python versions ([VQF](https://cris.fau.de/publications/284485551); [VQF docs](https://vqf.readthedocs.io/)). It can run on the Mac using the raw data you already stream.

**Calibration.** A one-time calibration removes the big constant errors. You set the sensor still in **12 different orientations** for a few seconds each, which takes under 2 minutes, with no special equipment ([Webering et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.04801v1); [Tedaldi et al. via imu_tk](https://gitee.com/ysfvest/imu_tk)). Gyro bias needs re-measuring at every power-up by averaging while still ([Woodman 2007](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/techreports/UCAM-CL-TR-696.pdf)). Bias also follows temperature, so log the chip's built-in temperature reading during the first 5–10 minutes of wear and subtract a fitted bias-vs-temperature line. On a test rig, warm-up bias settled in about 50 s ([MDPI Sensors 2023](https://mdpi.com/1424-8220/23/1/256)). Thermal calibration is a known, cheap fix ([Sensors 2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3821333/)).

**Chips.** The best real-world data comes from SlimeVR. Its open-source ESP32 body trackers do almost the same job as WristTap. Its community rates how long each chip lasts before heading needs a reset. The **MPU6500 lasts 5–10 minutes and the MPU6050 1–5 minutes**, both marked "avoid". The **ICM-45686 (about $6.70) lasts 45–60 minutes and the LSM6DSV (about $5.80) 45–50 minutes** ([SlimeVR IMU comparison](https://docs.slimevr.dev/diy/imu-comparison.html)). The BNO085 also reaches 45–60 minutes. But SlimeVR calls it discontinued, it misbehaves after sharp impacts (the "stomp bug", a bad sign for a tapping device), and fakes are common ([SlimeVR](https://docs.slimevr.dev/diy/imu-comparison.html)). It also has I2C timing problems on the ESP32, so it needs SPI wiring ([bno08x driver notes](https://github.com/bradcar/bno08x_i2c_spi_MicroPython)). In India a BNO085 breakout costs about **₹1,750–2,600** ([Hubtronics](https://hubtronics.in/peripheral-modules/bno085-9dof-orientation-imu-fusion); [Robu](https://st.robu.in/product/adafruit-9-dof-orientation-fusion-bno080-stemma-qt)). SlimeVR's ESP32 firmware is a ready code reference for ICM-45686 and LSM6DSV drivers and temperature calibration ([SlimeVR Tracker ESP](https://github.com/sojifes/SlimeVR-Tracker-ESP)).

**Magnetometers** (compasses) look like the obvious heading fix, but indoors they are unreliable. Steel, radiators and wiring bend the magnetic field ([Kok et al. 2013](https://people.isy.liu.se/rt/manko/publications/KokWSG2013.pdf)), and a projector and laptop sit right next to you. Even the BNO086's compass mode is less accurate during motion than its no-compass mode (**3.5° vs 2.5°**) ([SparkFun BNO086](https://www.sparkfun.com/products/retired/14686)). Use one only as a slow, double-checked backup, if at all.

**Stacking several IMUs** on one board and averaging them reduces noise by the square root of the count. One 8-gyro array patent reports **4.83× better bias stability** ([US 9772186](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9772186); [MIMU platform](https://typeset.io/pdf/an-open-source-multi-inertial-measurement-unit-mimu-platform-2p72z8xlkx.pdf)). It only helps after each sensor is calibrated, and one better chip is simpler.

**Deep learning** is the newest frontier, and it is improving fast. **ArmPoser** (Sept 2026) tracks the arm from one smartwatch with no calibration, at a median wrist error of **9–10 cm**. It runs live on a MacBook Air M3 ([ArmPoser](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.08806)). **HOOV** reaches **7.7 cm** ([HOOV](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.07016)). The best number, **2.33 cm**, needs a still torso, a known forward direction and known arm lengths ([Weigend et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.13192)). Those are the same conditions WristTap's arm model already uses directly. Learned gyro "denoising" is a cheaper idea worth knowing. A small network cleans gyro readings and beat camera-based systems on angle accuracy ([Brossard et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.10718)). But it needs accurate reference angles to train on. These models also learn from camera or motion-capture data during training, then run without cameras ([ArmPoser](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.08806)). A neat twist for WristTap: logged touches on known targets give perfect training labels for free. Finally, *detecting* a tap is a solved problem. Two wrist IMUs reached an **F1 score of 0.997** (near-perfect) on a table ([TapID](https://research-collection.ethz.ch/bitstream/handle/20.500.11850/488237/vr2021-TapID.pdf?sequence=3)). Your piezos should do at least as well.

## Ranking the options for a design student

Accuracy figures marked "est." are estimates built from the studies above, not measured on WristTap. "Wall error" means fingertip error on the projected wall at about 0.7 m reach.

| Rank | Option | What it fixes | Expected result | Cost | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Arm-angle model (forward kinematics) from wrist + upper-arm IMUs | Removes double integration entirely | ~2 cm right after calibration ([Sci Rep 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-01805-2)); drifts a few cm/min if never corrected (est.) | ₹0, hardware already built | Low |
| 2 | Calibration: 12-position accel, gyro bias at power-up, temperature table | Large constant errors, warm-up drift | Removes ±5 °/s-scale bias ([datasheet](https://ocw.cs.pub.ro/courses/_media/pm/prj2017/amocanu/mpu_6500_rev1.0_1_.pdf)); <2 min per sensor ([Webering](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.04801v1)) | ₹0 | Low |
| 3 | VQF filter with rest-based bias re-estimation | Tilt error, slows heading drift | ~2.9° average vs 5–17° for older filters ([VQF](https://cris.fau.de/publications/284485551)) | ₹0, open source | Low |
| 4 | Four-corner calibration + known-target touch resets + plane constraint | The drifting heading number, shoulder position, depth | Keeps error near the ~2 cm calibration level (est., untested anywhere) | ₹0 | Medium |
| 5 | Swap MPU6500 for ICM-45686 or LSM6DSV | Slower raw drift | Reset interval 5–10 min → 45–60 min ([SlimeVR](https://docs.slimevr.dev/diy/imu-comparison.html)) | ~$6–7 per chip ([SlimeVR](https://docs.slimevr.dev/diy/imu-comparison.html)) | Low–medium |
| 6 | Elbow-hinge constraint | Forearm vs upper-arm heading mismatch | <4° relative error ([Lehmann 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.00639)) | ₹0 | Medium–high (maths) |
| 7 | Chest IMU | Torso twist (~6 cm per 5° twist, est.) | Removes twist error; not body sway | ~$1–7 per chip ([SlimeVR](https://docs.slimevr.dev/diy/imu-comparison.html)) | Low |
| 8 | Small learned correction trained on your own touch logs | Leftover repeatable errors | Unknown; worth trying only after 1–7 | ₹0, Mac only | High |
| 9 | Magnetometer (BNO085, ICM-20948) | Long-term heading | Unreliable indoors ([Kok 2013](https://people.isy.liu.se/rt/manko/publications/KokWSG2013.pdf)) | ₹1,750–2,600 for BNO085 ([Hubtronics](https://hubtronics.in/peripheral-modules/bno085-9dof-orientation-imu-fusion)) | Medium |
| 10 | Multi-IMU array | Noise and bias via averaging | ~2–5× bias stability ([US 9772186](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9772186)) | Several chips + wiring | Medium |
| 11 | Full deep-learning arm pose (ArmPoser-style) | Everything, in one model | 7–10 cm wrist error ([ArmPoser](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.08806); [HOOV](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.07016)) | ₹0, but needs labelled data | High |
| 12 | Double integration / learned inertial odometry | Nothing usable here | Metres within a minute ([Woodman](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/techreports/UCAM-CL-TR-696.pdf)) | — | Reject |

Ranks 1–4 are the core system and cost nothing. Rank 5 is the one purchase clearly worth making, mainly because WristTap's current MPU6500 is the weakest common chip for heading. Until testing shows otherwise, design projected targets to be **at least 5–8 cm wide**. That size should stay hittable between touch resets. It is a design estimate, not a tested rule.

## A step-by-step plan from today's hardware

1. **Stop using position from acceleration.** In `wristtap.py`, compute hand position only from the two IMU angles plus arm lengths. Measure shoulder-to-elbow and elbow-to-fingertip with a tape for a first guess.
2. **Calibrate each IMU once.** Hold each band still in 12 different orientations for 2–3 s each. Fit accelerometer offsets and scale (see imu_tk). Save the results per sensor.
3. **Measure gyro bias at every start.** Ask the user to hold still for 3–5 s at power-up. Average the gyro and subtract it. Re-do this automatically whenever both IMUs look still.
4. **Log temperature during warm-up.** Wear the band for 10 minutes while logging gyro bias and the chip temperature at rest. Fit a straight line and subtract it. Add a thin foam layer between skin and sensor to slow heating.
5. **Run VQF on the Mac** (Python package) for each IMU, in 6-axis mode (no magnetometer). Feed it the calibrated data at 100–200 Hz.
6. **Build the four-corner calibration.** At each corner tap (piezo spike), save both IMU angles. Solve for shoulder position, heading offset and arm-length scale by least squares. Then map wall-plane points to projector pixels with a 4-point homography.
7. **Add touch resets.** On every piezo tap: (a) snap depth to the wall plane, (b) re-measure gyro bias during the still moment, (c) if the tap is on a known button, re-solve heading and shoulder position from that point. Blend each reset in gently over a few frames so the cursor does not jump.
8. **Measure your own accuracy.** Project a grid of dots. Tap them in random order every 10–30 s for 5 minutes. Log the gap between predicted and true positions over time. This one chart tells you whether touch resets keep the error flat. No published paper has this number.
9. **Upgrade the chips if drift is still too fast.** Replace both MPU6500s with ICM-45686 or LSM6DSV modules, and reuse SlimeVR's ESP32 drivers. A second sensor on one I2C bus can use address 0x69 (AD0 pin high). Check this against the datasheet of the chip you buy.
10. **Add a chest IMU** if the step-8 errors jump whenever the user turns their body. Express the arm angles relative to the chest.
11. **Add the elbow-hinge constraint** (Laidig/Lehmann method) if forearm and upper-arm headings slowly disagree.
12. **Only then try learning.** Use the logged touch data from step 8 (known target = true answer) to train a small model on the Mac. It should predict the *leftover* error, not the whole hand position.

## Conclusion

The big shift in thinking is this. For WristTap, drift is not a sensor-quality problem to brute-force. It is mostly one missing number, the arm's heading against the wall, and the interaction itself can supply it. Every touch on a known target measures exactly that number. So interface design and drift correction become the same job: where you place buttons, and how often the user taps them, decides how accurate the system stays. That is a design student's advantage, not a handicap.

The biggest open question is the one only you can answer. How fast does error grow between touches on your hardware, in your room? No study covers IMU-only wall touch, so the step-8 grid test is real research. If it shows the error staying flat at a few centimetres with touches every 10–30 s, that result is publishable at a CHI or UIST-style venue in its own right.
