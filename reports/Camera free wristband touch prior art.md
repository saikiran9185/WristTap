# Let the band feel touches, let the room say where

Nobody has built what WristTap is aiming for. No published research system or product finds the absolute position of a hand on a wall-sized projection using only a wearable, with no camera. The pattern across 20 years of research is consistent. Wrist sensors are very good at **when** a touch happens (about 95–99% reliable, roughly 10 ms delay) and at **which** gesture the hand makes (about 90–97% on 5–10 gestures, usually after training on one person). They are poor at **where**, because no sensor on the body can feel which way the body faces in the room. Even Meta's 2026 research wristband, which packs in seven kinds of sensor, only tracks *relative* motion, like a laptop trackpad. That leaves WristTap with two honest paths. **Band only**, it can reach a few centimetres of accuracy right after you tap a known spot. That error grows as the arm's compass direction drifts, so the interface must use big targets of about 10–25 cm and treat every touch as a reset. **With a small, cheap add-on in the room**, it can get a real absolute position that works in the dark. The best options are four piezo discs on a hard projection panel (about 2.5–4 cm, under Rs 1,500), a few speakers sending near-ultrasonic chirps to a mic on the band, or UWB radio anchors (about 10 cm, Rs 10–25k). The recommended build is a band that handles WHEN, WHAT and a rough WHERE. It sends data by ESP-NOW radio to a USB dongle on the Mac. A Mac app turns that into cursor clicks and OSC messages. The room add-on is left until you have measured how far the band-only estimate drifts.

## Wrist sensors answer "when" and "what", something else answers "where"

It helps to split WristTap's goal into three questions. WHEN did the hand touch something? WHAT gesture is the hand making? WHERE is it? Every system below answers some of these. None answers all three on a wall from the wrist alone.

The closest prior art to WristTap's piezo discs is **Acustico** (Facebook Reality Labs and Dartmouth, UIST 2020). It puts 4 vibration sensors on the wrist: 2 accelerometers and 2 microphones. A tap reaches each sensor at a slightly different moment, and the device works out the tap's position from those time gaps. This method is called **TDOA** (time difference of arrival). Acustico detected taps with an F1 score of **0.9987** (F1 is a 0–1 accuracy score where 1 is perfect). It located them to about **7.6 mm by 4.6 mm** across 20 people ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3379337.3415901); [Meta Research](https://research.facebook.com/publications/acustico-surface-tap-detection-and-localization-using-wrist-based-acoustic-tdoa-sensing/)). The catch is that the position is the fingertip *relative to the wrist*, on a small patch near a hand resting on the surface ([Hackster](https://www.hackster.io/news/tapping-into-vibrations-9a684dfb2a65)). WristTap's 4 piezos could do the same job: tell which finger tapped, or how far the fingertip is from the wrist. They cannot tell where the wrist is on the wall.

The body-sensing classics show the same limit. **Skinput** (Microsoft Research, 2010) used an armband of vibration sensors plus a tiny projector to turn the forearm into a touchscreen. It recognised up to 5 tap locations at up to **95.5%** ([MSR PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/p111-harrison.pdf)). **ViBand** (CMU, UIST 2016) sped up a smartwatch accelerometer to **4 kHz** (4,000 readings a second) and classified 17 gestures at **94.3%**. Each user trained it with 15 examples per gesture ([ViBand PDF](https://modernmobile.cs.washington.edu/docs/viband.pdf)). Both pick from a small set of fixed spots. Neither gives a smooth, continuous position.

Meta's **TouchFusion** wristband (arXiv, Feb 2026) is the strongest recent evidence. It fuses muscle signals (sEMG), skin electrical impedance, infrared proximity, two depth sensors, an IMU and a magnetometer. It detects touching the world with AUC-ROC **0.994** (another 0–1 score) and succeeds on a median **95%** of live taps. Yet it tracks the fingertip only as relative motion. Its pointing speed is just **1.1–1.2 bits/s**, far below a mouse. It makes no claim to know absolute position ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.15011)). If Meta's kitchen-sink band does not try absolute position, a band with two IMUs cannot simply out-sense the problem.

Shipping products tell the same story. Each one either recognises a few gestures or moves a relative cursor, like an air mouse.

| Product | How it senses | What it gives | Absolute position? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Meta Neural Band (2025) | Muscle signals (sEMG) | 9 gestures, >90% on new users; handwriting 20.9 WPM ([Nature](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v645y2025i8081d10.1038_s41586-025-09255-w.html)) | No |

The Wii Remote is the useful contrast. It only pointed absolutely because a small camera inside it watched two infrared lights on the sensor bar. Without the bar, it fell back to relative motion ([UMD study](https://core.ac.uk/download/56107538.pdf)). Every absolute system needs a fixed reference point in the room. WristTap has to find a reference that is not a camera.

Two products also show traps to avoid. Tap Strap's mouse mode kept sensing the table up to about 2 inches away, so it did not switch off when the hand lifted. Users also found that the same wrist motion both moved the pointer and clicked, which made clicks jerk the cursor ([Wired mirror](https://wirednews.pages.dev/review/tap-strap-2); [Apple World Today](https://appleworld.today/2019/10/tap-strap-2-airmouse-improves-on-the-first-version-but-is-still-tricky-to-master/)). WristTap's FSRs and piezo spikes give a clear "really touching now" signal, which avoids both problems.

The nearest attempts at large-display pointing are smartwatch arm models. **Watchpoint** (MobileHCI 2016) pointed and clicked on large displays with a watch and matched other systems on speed and errors ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2909132.2909263)). **ArmPoser** (2026) estimates arm pose from one watch IMU but states plainly that it **cannot recover absolute heading**, the arm's compass direction in the room ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.08806)). **SmartPoser** (2025) fixes this by adding UWB radio ranging between a phone and a watch ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.03451)). That is the pattern in a nutshell: IMUs for shape, an outside radio for the absolute anchor.

## Band alone can reach a few centimetres, but only right after a touch

WristTap's band-only strategy is already the right shape. Do not add up acceleration to get position. That "double integration" error reaches metres in seconds. Instead, place the hand using the upper-arm angle, the forearm angle and the two lengths, like a drawing compass. This is called forward kinematics. A 2021 study using this two-IMU arm model measured wrist position relative to the shoulder to about **±1.8 cm** against a lab camera system ([Sci Rep 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-01805-2)). Published smartwatch arm tracking does worse over a whole session: WearMoCap reports **6.8 cm** wrist error in upper-arm mode and **10–12 cm** in other modes ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11738771/)).

The gap between 2 cm and 10 cm is mostly one number: heading, also called yaw. Gravity tells each IMU which way is down, so tilt never drifts. Nothing in a 6-axis IMU can sense which way you face, so heading slowly creeps. The geometry is simple arithmetic. With a 0.7 m reach, **each degree of heading error moves the fingertip about 1.2 cm** sideways on the wall. Hobby IMUs drift about 0.5–1.8 degrees per minute, so expect a few centimetres of sideways creep per minute without corrections ([Parallax forum](https://forums.parallax.com/uploads/attachments/41167/106673.pdf); [SparkFun BNO086](https://www.sparkfun.com/products/retired/14686)). A magnetometer (compass chip) does not help much indoors, because projectors, laptops and steel in walls bend the magnetic field.

The fix is to treat every touch as a free "you are here" signal. Every touch says the fingertip is on the wall plane, which fixes depth. Every touch also says the hand is still for a moment, so the gyro's false reading (bias) can be re-measured. Only a touch on a **known** spot, such as a projected button, fixes heading itself. So the interface design is part of the tracking system. Big buttons at known places, and the occasional "tap here to continue", keep the error near its post-calibration level.

Some limits cannot be fixed from the arm. If the user steps sideways or leans, the shoulder moves, and wrist and upper-arm IMUs cannot see it. A floor marker to stand on, plus a re-calibration tap after moving, is the practical answer. For 3D hand position in the room while hovering, band-only gives a good shape relative to the shoulder but no true room position. Even neural-network inertial tracking drifts by **3.5–6.5% of distance travelled** ([TLIO arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01867)). ETH's 2026 Ultra Diffusion Poser added UWB distances to its IMUs precisely because "IMUs give no absolute measurement" ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.02153)). No paper reports wall-touch error over time for a two-IMU arm chain, so WristTap will have to measure its own.

## A small room add-on turns a rough guess into a real position

If band-only accuracy is not enough, the next step is a little infrastructure in the room. Every option below works in total darkness and uses no camera or lidar. They differ hugely in accuracy, cost and effort.

| Option | What it gives | Accuracy (cited) | What goes in the room | Rough cost (India) | Effort (1 easy, 5 hard) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| IMU arm model + touch resets | Hand position relative to shoulder | ~2 cm just after a reset; 6.8–12 cm over a session | Nothing | Rs 500–2,000 | 2 |
| **4 piezos on a hard wall panel** | Touch point x,y only | σ 2.5 cm on glass, 3–4 cm thicker glass | Glass, acrylic or MDF panel + 4 discs | Rs 200–1,500 | 3 |
| **Speaker chirps to a mic on the band** | Absolute 3D | ~10 cm (Cricket); mm-level in research | 3–4 speakers or tweeters | Rs 500–2,000 | 4 |
| **UWB radio (DW3000)** | Absolute 3D | ~10 cm; 10–15 cm DIY | 4–6 powered anchors | Rs 10k–25k | 3 |
| Marvelmind ultrasonic kit | Absolute 3D | ±2 cm | 4+ beacons | ~Rs 60–80k (est., imported) | 2 (buy) |
| Projector light code + photodiode | Exact projector pixel | ~1 pixel | The projector itself | Rs 50 | 2 |
| mmWave radar (4 units) | Touch x,y | 1.6 cm (90% of touches) | 4 radar boards | High | 5 |
| Magnetic coils (DIY, room) | Absolute 3D | 15–30 cm measured | Coils | Rs 1–3k | 4 |
| Wi-Fi signals (CSI/FTM) | Gesture type; metres | Not cm-level | Routers/radios | Low–high | 4–5 |

**Wall-tap acoustics is the most direct fix for WHERE you press.** It ignores the band's position entirely and listens to the wall. MIT's Responsive Window put 4 piezo pickups near the corners of a glass pane. It located knocks to **σ = 2.5 cm on tempered glass** over areas up to 2 × 2 m, in about 65 ms (σ, "sigma", means most hits fall within that distance) ([MIT Media Lab](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/passive-acoustic-knock-tracking-for-interactive-windows-2); [Sensor Review 2005](https://d2pnhgckm19p0r.cloudfront.net/insight/content/doi/10.1108/02602280510585727/full/html)). A table-sized TDOA system reached about 3 cm ([MoBat, TUM](https://www.campar.in.tum.de/pub/kreskowski2015mobat/kreskowski2015mobat.pdf)). The weakness is the surface. Vibrations need a hard, continuous panel. A painted plaster or brick wall soaks them up. The same MoBat team found piezos in a chipboard table too noisy. No accuracy figures exist for plaster walls. The cheap fix is a 4 × 3 ft acrylic, glass or MDF sheet as the projection screen, with a disc in each corner. Timing must be fast. By my rough maths, 2.5 cm needs around 40,000 samples a second per channel and 1 cm needs over 100,000. A multi-channel USB audio interface on the Mac, or comparator timing chips, handles that better than the ESP32's built-in ADC. In this setup the band still matters. It confirms which person tapped and with which gesture and press strength.

**Speaker chirps give absolute 3D hover cheaply, if near-ultrasound is acceptable.** Ultrasound is the most accurate option that uses neither radio nor light. MIT's Cricket timed radio plus ultrasound pulses and got about **5 cm distance and 10 cm position** ([Priyantha thesis](https://people.csail.mit.edu/rudolph/Teaching/Articles/bodhi-thesis.pdf)). Research systems using swept "chirp" tones on phone speakers reach millimetres. MilliSonic reports **2.6 mm** median 3D error with a 4-mic array ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1901.06601)). A DIY WristTap version would place 3–4 small tweeters around the projection. They play 18–22 kHz chirps, a small I2S digital mic on the band records them, and the radio carries the timing sync. The parts cost very little. The risks are real, though. The body must not block the line from mic to speakers. Young people can sometimes hear 18–22 kHz. Laptop speakers sit too close together for good room geometry. No source confirms how well an ESP32 mic captures those frequencies, so test that first.

**UWB is the sturdiest radio option, but too coarse on its own.** UWB (ultra-wideband) radio times very short pulses between the band and fixed "anchors" around the room. A peer-reviewed ESP32 + DWM3000 system reached about **10 cm** with five anchors ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.10194v1)). DIY builds report **10–15 cm** in good conditions at about 10 updates a second ([CircuitDigest](https://circuitdigest.com/microcontrollers-projects/diy-indoor-uwb-positioning-system-using-esp32-and-qorvo-dwm3000)). Modules cost about Rs 2,100–3,300 each in India ([DigiKey IN](https://www.digikey.in/en/products/detail/qorvo/DWM3000TR13/24367320); [Tanotis](https://www.tanotis.com/products/qorvo-dwm3000tr13-transceiver-module-gpio-spi-8-25-ghz-6-8-mbps-55-ma-2-5-to-3-6v)). Ten centimetres is about the size of a projected button, so UWB cannot pick buttons alone. Its value is that it never drifts. Blending it with the IMU arm model gives you both: IMU for smooth, fine shape, UWB to stop the heading creep. The body sitting between the band and the anchors is the main real-world error, so mount anchors high in the corners.

**The projector itself can find the band, if a little flashing is allowed.** This is light, but not a camera. In 2004, Johnny Lee and colleagues flashed black-and-white stripe patterns ("Gray codes") from a projector. A light sensor read the on/off sequence and learned the exact projector pixel it sat on ([MERL TR2004-036](https://merl.com/publications/docs/TR2004-036.pdf)). About 20 patterns cover a 1024 × 768 image ([Hackaday](https://hackaday.com/2007/11/15/automatic-projector-calibration)). A photodiode on the band near the fingertip costs about Rs 50. On each tap, the Mac could flash the 20 frames, about a third of a second at 60 Hz, and get pixel-perfect wall coordinates. It works best in a dark room. The costs are a visible flicker on each touch and a sensor that must sit inside the projector beam. If the "no light" rule is about cameras and lidar rather than the projector, this is the cheapest perfect answer.

The rest fit poorly. Commercial magnetic trackers like Polhemus are sub-millimetre but very expensive and short-range ([Liberty datasheet](https://home.iitm.ac.in/skm/manuals/LIBERTY.pdf)). DIY room-scale magnetic systems measure only **15–30 cm** ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.00817v1)). Magnets work well at hand scale, though. Finexus tracked fingertips to about **1 mm** with 4 magnetometers ([UW Ubicomp](https://ubicomplab.cs.washington.edu/publications/finexus)), which suits finger pose, not room position. Radar can locate wall touches to **1.6 cm** with 4 units, but it is costly and complex ([Amazon Science](https://cdn.amazon.science/25/4f/ffc25e4b4171bdda21f99581c2e6/sub-resolution-mmwave-fmcw-radar-based-touch-localization-using-deep-learning.pdf)). Wi-Fi signals on an ESP32 can classify gestures at 92–97% but cannot place a hand to the centimetre ([Preprints](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202602.0018)).

## The band's own sensors cover touch, press and gestures well

WristTap's sensor mix can already answer WHEN and WHAT. Each sensor has a clear job.

**Piezos detect the moment of touch.** A piezo disc only reacts to *change*, so it shows a sharp spike when a finger hits a surface and a smaller one on release. It cannot tell you that a finger is still pressing. Tap vibrations travel through the arm best between **20 Hz and 1 kHz**, with peaks near 170 Hz and 750 Hz ([ViBand PDF](https://modernmobile.cs.washington.edu/docs/viband.pdf)). WristTap's current 500 readings a second captures only frequencies below 250 Hz. That is enough to notice *that* a tap happened and how hard. It is not enough to tell a flick from a snap or a knuckle from a fingertip. The cheap upgrade is to sample the piezos at 2,000 or more readings a second. Alternatively, add a simple analog low-pass filter at about 200–250 Hz so higher frequencies do not fold back in as noise ("aliasing").

**FSRs tell you how hard you press and whether you are still pressing.** When fingers press, the forearm tendons bulge under the strap, and the FSRs feel it. This is called force myography (FMG). MIT's WristFlex used wrist FSRs to tell subtle pinches apart at over 80% ([MIT Media Lab](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/wristflex-low-power-gesture-input-with-wrist-worn-pressure-sensors/)). Research bands reach 87–97%, but they use **8–16 FSRs** and are trained per user. Accuracy drops when the band is taken off and put back on. A 16-FSR band scored 96.7% in testing but 89.4% across separate trials ([PubMed](https://www.pubmed.gov/28161107/)). Two FSRs will not identify individual fingers. They will give a solid "light, firm, not pressing" signal and a sustained "still touching" state. Wrist angle changes FSR readings, so the model needs wrist orientation as an input ([PMC6199756](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6199756)). Google's EI-Lite estimated pinch force from the wrist to about **0.55 N** ([Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/ei-lite-electrical-impedance-sensing-for-micro-gesture-recognition-and-pinch-force-estimation/)). That is a good best-case target, which means 3–5 press-strength levels in the UI.

**IMUs add gesture shape and a second tap signal.** A smartwatch accelerometer and gyro alone recognised 5 finger gestures at 87% across users and near-perfectly per user ([Serendipity](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/301931139_Serendipity_Finger_Gesture_Recognition_using_an_Off-the-Shelf_Smartwatch)). Watch accelerometers scored **98.2% on 5 thumb gestures but 80.1% on 17** ([Tsunoda, MUM 2024](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3701571.3701600)). So keep the gesture vocabulary to about **5–8 gestures**. An IMU ring on the index finger raised contact detection to an F1 of 98.6% at about 10 ms ([Gu et al.](https://xiaoying-wei.github.io/TappingRing.pdf)). That is a strong case for moving the second IMU from the upper arm to a finger, if pointing accuracy turns out not to need the upper-arm sensor.

Put together, the touch logic is a simple chain. A piezo or IMU spike means touch-down. FSR load staying above its resting level means still touching. FSR size means press strength. Falling load plus a release spike means lift-off. Hovering produces no spike and little tendon load, so firm taps separate cleanly from hover, while feather-light touches stay fuzzy. Meta's TouchFusion failed mostly on *missed* touch onsets ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.15011v1)), and WristTap will likely see the same.

For the software, start simple. ViBand's method works at this scale: turn a short window of data into frequency features, then feed them to an SVM (a simple, classic classifier). Train it with 15 examples per gesture. The single biggest fix for false alarms was recording lots of "nothing happening" data while walking, typing and washing hands. With that, ViBand logged only **6 false positives in 77 minutes** across 17 users ([ViBand PDF](https://modernmobile.cs.washington.edu/docs/viband.pdf)). Move to a small neural network (1D-CNN plus LSTM, as Meta uses) only after collecting an hour or two of labelled data. Report accuracy across sessions and across people, not just within one session. The Tomo wristband scored 97% for one user in one session, yet similar systems fell to **39–52%** on new users ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2807442.2807480); [EITPose](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3613904.3642663)). A webcam can still help *during training only*. MediaPipe hand landmarks can label pinches and finger angles while recording band data, as WatchHand and EtherPose did ([EtherPose](https://www.hackster.io/news/radio-killed-the-video-stream-98b0b768474d)). The finished band then runs with no camera.

## An ESP-NOW dongle is the fastest, simplest link to the Mac

The band currently streams 500 packets a second over a USB cable, about 160 kbit/s. Going wireless, the main choices differ mostly in delay. A controlled benchmark measured **ESP-NOW at about 5–6 ms** for a single packet. ESP-NOW is Espressif's direct board-to-board radio, which needs no router. Tuned Wi-Fi measured 6–9 ms, but default Wi-Fi settings gave **74–84 ms**. WebSockets measured about 14 ms and ESP32 Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) 14–22 ms ([Electric UI](https://www.electricui.com/blog/latency-comparison)). BLE to a Mac is also unpredictable at high rates. Apple engineers say the app is "stuck with whatever iOS will accept". An M3 MacBook managed only an unstable 400–600 kbps from a device that did 1,000+ kbps on Android ([Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/770717)).

The recommendation is **ESP-NOW from the band to a second ESP32 plugged into the Mac's USB**. That second board, the "dongle", outputs the same serial data the Mac app already reads. It needs no venue Wi-Fi, which matters at exhibitions, and it avoids Mac Bluetooth quirks. SlimeVR, the open-source body-tracking project, moved from Wi-Fi to a USB dongle for the same reasons ([SlimeVR docs](https://docs.slimevr.dev/smol-slimes/)). Turn Wi-Fi power saving off, because users report heavy ESP-NOW packet loss otherwise ([ESP-IDF FAQ](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-faq/en/latest/software-framework/protocols/other-protocols.html)). Put a microsecond timestamp and a counter in every packet so the Mac can spot lost packets and line up timing. A BLE mouse mode is a nice "works anywhere" demo, but macOS reliably accepts only a *relative* mouse over Bluetooth. A touchscreen-style "digitizer" description that worked on Windows did nothing on macOS 14.7 ([Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/687740)).

Battery size follows from the radio. ESP-NOW with power saving off keeps the receiver on, roughly 100–150 mA for the whole band. A 3-hour session then needs about a **500 mAh** pouch cell, roughly 5 × 30 × 40 mm. BLE that sends only finished events would need about 250–300 mAh. These are estimates, so measure with a USB power meter. The ESP32-C3 or C6 are the wearable picks, being small, low-power and BLE 5 capable. Boards like the XIAO ESP32C3 include a battery charger. If you use a TP4056 charger board instead, change its resistor. Stock boards charge at 1 A, too fast for small cells, and about 5–6 kΩ gives a safe 200–240 mA ([TP4056 datasheet](https://datasheet.lcsc.com/lcsc/2311091738_UMW-Youtai-Semiconductor-Co---Ltd--TP4056_C725790.pdf)). Use the IMU's motion interrupt to wake the band from deep sleep, which draws only about 7 µA ([Deneyapkart](https://deneyapkart.org/en/docs/guc-tuketimi-esp32-s3)).

On the Mac, a small app receives packets, smooths them, and maps wall coordinates to projector pixels. The mapping uses a **homography**, a 3 × 3 transform built from 4 calibration taps on projected corner targets. OpenCV's `getPerspectiveTransform` does it in one line, and `findHomography` fits 5–9 targets and reports the leftover error ([OpenCV](https://docs.opencv.org/4.x/da/d54/group__imgproc__transform.html)). That leftover error, in centimetres, doubles as your accuracy score. The app then either moves the real cursor and clicks with CGEvents, or sends OSC or WebSocket messages to TouchDesigner or a browser page. CGEvents need the Accessibility permission in System Settings and do not work in sandboxed apps ([Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/103992)). One app can do both at once.

## Design the interface around a pointer that misses by centimetres

Treat WristTap as a coarse pointer and make the interface absorb the error. Even a camera-based touch system needs targets about three times its average error to catch 95% of touches. HoloLens MRTouch had 5.4 mm average error and needed 16 mm buttons ([MRTouch](https://robertxiao.ca/pubs/2018_IEEEVR_MRTouch.pdf)). Apply the same ratio to WristTap. An average error of 3–8 cm means targets of roughly **9–24 cm** across. On a 2 m wide projection that is 4–10 columns at most. Think 2 × 3 to 3 × 4 tile grids, radial menus and targets along the edges.

Use a **bubble cursor**. Instead of a point that must land inside a button, the cursor always grabs the *nearest* target. Each button's clickable zone becomes its whole share of the wall, while the drawn button stays modest. In the original study it clearly beat a normal point cursor ([Grossman & Balakrishnan, CHI 2005](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/papers/tgrossman_CHI2005.pdf)). Add "stickiness": once a target is grabbed, the estimate must move clearly into a neighbour's zone before switching, so noise does not make the highlight flicker. Confirm with the physical tap, which is a crisp, intentional click. Do not use "hover and wait", which triggers things by accident. Show where the system thinks the hand is: a soft projected blob sized to its current confidence, a highlight on the grabbed target, and ideally a short buzz from a small vibration motor. Let gestures carry the precise commands, such as swipe for next, double-tap for back and press-and-hold for menus. Then pointing only has to choose among a few big things. Every tap on a known button also re-anchors the drift, so a good layout literally makes the tracking better.

## Verdict, recommended architecture and step-by-step plan

Here is the honest verdict. **Band only**, WristTap can reliably deliver WHEN (touch-down, lift-off, press strength) and WHAT (5–8 gestures, trained per user). It can also give a WHERE good enough for big-tile interfaces, at a few centimetres just after each known-target tap. That error grows with time between taps and jumps if the user moves their feet. It cannot give true absolute 3D room position or reliably hit small targets. That is not a design flaw. It is the physics of IMUs, and no published system has beaten it. **Band plus minimal infrastructure** changes the picture. A 4-piezo hard projection panel gives a touch point accurate to about 2.5–4 cm that never drifts, for under Rs 1,500. Speakers plus a band mic, or UWB anchors, add absolute 3D hover. Projector light codes give pixel-perfect taps if a brief flicker is acceptable. Showing band-only first and adding one cheap reference later is a strong story in its own right. It matches the field's consensus, and the first system is honest about what it cannot do.

The recommended architecture has four parts. The **band** is an ESP32-C3 or C6 with the wrist IMU, piezos sampled at 2 kHz or more, 2 FSRs over the palm-side tendons, a small vibration motor and a ~500 mAh cell. The **link** is ESP-NOW to an ESP32 dongle on the Mac's USB. The **Mac app** runs the arm model with touch resets, the gesture classifier and touch state machine, and the 4-point homography. It sends CGEvent clicks plus OSC or WebSocket messages. The **projected interface** uses big tiles, a bubble cursor and tap to confirm. One room add-on can be slotted in later, chosen by measured need.

| Step | What to build | How you know it worked |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Piezo tap detection: raise piezo sampling to 2 kHz+ (or add a 200–250 Hz low-pass), detect spikes above a self-adjusting noise floor | Taps caught within ~30 ms; few false alarms while walking and typing |
| 2 | FSR press: record a resting baseline each time the band goes on; calibrate press strength against a kitchen scale in 2–3 wrist angles | 3–5 distinct press levels; clean "still touching" state |
| 3 | Arm model: orientation filter on both IMUs, forearm + upper-arm lengths, standing marker on the floor | Hand moves smoothly on screen as the arm moves |
| 4 | Calibration and mapping: tap 4 projected corners, then 9 test targets; build the homography | Average and worst-case error in cm across the wall |
| 5 | Touch resets: on each tap of a known target, correct heading and gyro bias | Error after 5 minutes of use stays near the step-4 level |
| 6 | Gestures: pick 5–8; record 15–30 examples each plus lots of "nothing" data; features + SVM; test on a different day | ≥95% within a session; cross-day result reported honestly |
| 7 | Wireless: ESP-NOW band to dongle, timestamps and counters, power saving off; battery + charger + deep sleep | <10 ms delay, no visible packet loss, 3 h on a charge |
| 8 | Mac app outputs: CGEvent mouse plus OSC/WebSocket; big-tile UI with bubble cursor, confidence blob and buzz on grab | A stranger can pick tiles reliably in a dark room |
| 9 | Decide on an add-on from the step 4–5 numbers: if error stays under ~5 cm, stop; if touch point is the issue, build the 4-piezo panel; if hover position matters, test speaker chirps or UWB | The chosen add-on beats band-only on the same 9-target test |

## Conclusion

The research reframes WristTap's ambition. The novel part is not "a band that knows where it is". The field is clear that no band can know that alone. The novel part is a band that turns every touch into both a command and a position fix, inside an interface built so those fixes happen naturally. Nobody has published wall-scale touch accuracy for a wrist wearable, and no standard metric exists for it. So WristTap's most valuable output may be a measurement. That means average and worst-case centimetre error on a grid of projected targets, how it drifts over five minutes, and how much one cheap room reference improves it. A clear, honest error map is exactly the kind of evidence an HCI lab or jury rewards. It also tells you, with data rather than guesswork, whether the room add-on is needed at all.
